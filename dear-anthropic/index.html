01 / The shift

Search is the scaffolding we use to navigate knowledge.

Search is also one of the web’s deepest habits. Google now handles more than 5 trillion searches a year through an interface that feels familiar, trusted, and effortless.

But great search was never just a ranking problem. In Delphic Costs and Benefits in Web Search, Broder and McAfee argue that search quality is really about reducing the total cognitive, interaction, and time costs required to reach a useful outcome. That is the right frame for AI, too. Users do not care whether retrieval was technically elegant. They care whether the system helps them get somewhere useful, quickly, clearly, and with enough confidence to act.

That distinction matters because benchmark performance is not the same as product value. BrowseComp tests whether a system can locate a single hard-to-find fact, and its creators explicitly note that it is not designed to measure common open-ended queries. DeepSearchQA measures something different again: exhaustive, multi-step research across open-ended search spaces. Both are useful. Neither is the same as daily search, where the job is often simpler and harder at once: reduce the costs of deciding. Help me decide faster, with less effort, in the right format, and with enough trust to move.

This is why search matters so much now. It is the surface where models earn trust, become habit, and shape a user’s relationship with reality.

02 / The wedge

The wedge is trust, earned through habit.

Anthropic has said Claude should be “a trusted tool for thought.” But trust like that is rarely granted all at once. It is built through repeated success on smaller, everyday questions, the moments that teach users when a system is useful, when it is reliable, and when it is worth returning to.

That is why search matters strategically. If Claude is going to be the system people trust for questions that are consequential, confusing, or expensive, it first has to become the place they turn when the stakes are low. Habit is what builds the trust that later matters under pressure.

The wedge, then, is not generic assistant utility. It is becoming the default starting point for everyday uncertainty, and using that repeated contact to earn the right to help when the stakes are real.

This is a particularly strong wedge for Anthropic. Claude already signals care, caution, and seriousness more credibly than most AI products. Search can turn those qualities into a durable advantage by making research feel calm, legible, and dependable.

If Anthropic gets this right, it does not just gain more usage. It becomes the system people trust when the answer actually matters.

03 / The information ecosystem

Search sits on top of the information ecosystem.

Great search does not begin with the model. It begins with the information ecosystem the model sits on top of. If the underlying supply of information is weak, inaccessible, poorly attributed, or structurally incomplete, the search experience will be weaker too.

The first layer is access. The BBC’s research on AI assistants is useful here because it shows that quality problems are often inseparable from sourcing problems. If assistants cannot reliably access high-quality information, or if they present it without enough attribution, context, or clarity, trust breaks down quickly. Search quality depends on the health of the information supply chain, not just the intelligence of the interface.

The second layer is structure. Many of the most valuable search experiences do not depend on prose alone. They depend on machine-readable data: product specs, pricing, reviews, merchant feeds, availability, opening hours, maps, and local business information. This is what turns search from a text experience into a useful one. It is also what underpins agentic commerce, local discovery, and many of the workflows where AI can actually help people do something rather than just read something.

That is why building a great search experience requires grappling with both access and structure. Access determines whether the system can reach trustworthy information in the first place. Structure determines whether it can turn that information into something useful, legible, and actionable.

In other words: search is not just a model problem or a retrieval problem. It is an ecosystem problem.

04 / The UX of search

Search is not one interface. It is a grammar of result types.

Over two decades, Google layered rich result types on top of the classical ten blue links: maps, shopping, news, weather, videos, recipes, and more. That mattered because format is part of meaning. The way information is presented shapes how quickly people can scan it, how much confidence they place in it, and what they feel ready to do next.

Those formats did not emerge by accident. They were built through a combination of product design, ranking systems, and structured data that allowed information to be parsed and presented in consistent ways. Search became more useful not just because it found the right information, but because it learned how to present different kinds of information in the right form.

AI opens up a larger design space again: comparisons, guided flows, step-by-step responses, interactive explanations, diagrams, and other formats that do more than summarize. But that makes the UX problem harder, not easier. Matching the interface to the intent requires judgment, and consistency matters as much as novelty.

Anthropic should treat search UX as a core product capability. The winning system will not just generate strong answers. It will know when to answer, when to show, when to compare, when to guide, and when to preserve the structure of the underlying information.

05 / Metrics, evals, provenance

Search quality should be measured like a trust system, not just a relevance system.

All of the classical metrics still matter: coverage, freshness, latency, relevance, task completion. But AI search adds a harder requirement. It is not enough to retrieve something useful. The system also has to help the user decide whether the answer is current, well-supported, inspectable, and safe to act on.

Mind2Web 2 usefully frames this as an evaluation crisis for agentic search: once answers become long-horizon, time-varying, and citation-backed, conventional benchmarks stop being enough. That changes what should be measured. In addition to relevance, Anthropic should care about confidence calibration, source diversity, citation usefulness, update sensitivity, and whether users can actually interrogate the basis of an answer rather than merely receive it.

This is where provenance stops being a garnish layer and becomes part of the product. Good provenance is not just a list of links appended to an answer. It is an interface for inspection: evidence presented clearly enough that a user can verify, compare, and keep thinking.

Since Anthropic relies on third-party web indexing infrastructure, it also needs strong internal systems for auditing source quality, inspecting retrieval behavior, and ensuring the product is not confidently citing spam, low-quality pages, or manipulated results.

A serious search organization would treat evals, provenance design, and information access as one system. The goal is not to maximize visible citations but rather to ensure truth-seeking behavior is easy and transparent.
